Fluid Mechanics · AI Project

Why a calligraphy pen writes thin on the way up — and thick on the way down

This is an AI project exploring AI capabilities: the page and nib-flow calculator were built first with Muse from the group's own prompts, then a second AI model — Claude — built its own nib model, and the best of Claude's work was merged back into this page and checked against hand-verified fluid mechanics. The physics question underneath is simple — ink reaches the paper through a slit only about 30 micrometres tall, so what actually pushes it there, and what happens when pressure on the pen flexes that slit open?

98 PaGravity head of a 1 cm ink column — capillary wins by ~29×
3.17 µL/sNormal writing flow rate, at Reynolds number 0.79 (deeply laminar)
4×, not 8×Flow gain when the gap doubles — the flex-pen trap, explained below

The one-minute version

A pointed nib is two tines with a narrow slit between them. Surface tension pulls ink down that slit far harder than gravity does, so the pen works at almost any angle. Flow through the slit follows the parallel-plate form of the Hagen–Poiseuille law, where flow rate grows with the cube of the gap height. On a light upstroke the tines stay closed and the line is hair-thin. On a heavy downstroke the tines flex apart — but as the gap widens, the capillary suction that drives the flow falls in proportion. The two effects partly cancel, which is why doubling the gap gives about four times the flow, not eight.

Try it in the live demo: drag the gap slider from 30 µm to 60 µm and watch the flow rate, then switch on the pressurised-cartridge mode to see the case where the simple equation stops being valid altogether.

The physics — course content

Three ideas from the course do all the work: capillary pressure (Young–Laplace), viscous flow between parallel plates (Hagen–Poiseuille), and the Reynolds-number check that tells you whether the second one is allowed.

ΔPcap = 2γ·cosθ / h Capillary suction. γ is surface tension, θ the ink–nib contact angle, h the slit gap. Narrower gap → stronger pull. This is why the pen works upside down: at h = 30 µm it produces 2,819 Pa against gravity's 98 Pa for a 1 cm ink column (ρgh).
Q = w·h³·ΔP / (12·μ·L) Parallel-plate Hagen–Poiseuille. w is slit width, L slit length, μ ink viscosity. Note the h³: a slit is not a round pipe, so the pipe formula (πD⁴ΔP / 128μL) must not be used — that swap is one of the classic errors graded in the model comparison.
Re = ρ·v·h / μ,   v = Q / (w·h) Validity check. The flow equation assumes laminar flow. Normal writing gives Re ≈ 0.79 — safely laminar. If a case pushes Re toward 1,000 or beyond, the honest output is a warning, not a number.
x(t) = √( 2·K·ΔP·t / μ ),   K = h²·(1 − 0.63·h/w) / 12 Washburn filling — added from the second model's build. Before the slit is full, the ink front at position x obeys x·dx/dt = K·ΔP/μ, so the front advances with √t and the time to fill the whole slit is tfill = L²μ / (2K·ΔP) — about 19 ms at the normal-writing settings. The side view and position graph in the demo animate exactly this.
Qcorr = Q · (1 − 0.63·h/w),   Dh = 2wh / (w+h),   Ca = μ·v / γ Refinements from the second model. The parallel-plate formula assumes an infinitely wide slit; the factor (1 − 0.63·h/w) corrects for the side walls — at the benchmark geometry (h/w = 0.015) it is 0.99, a ~1% refinement, which is why the graded benchmark values above are unchanged. The second model also wrote capillary pressure as γ·cosθ·(2/h + 2/w), counting the slit's side walls as well as its top and bottom (2,861 Pa vs 2,819 Pa at benchmark — same conclusion, ~1.5% apart), and based its Reynolds number on the hydraulic diameter Dh ≈ 2h rather than h, which roughly doubles its Re. Both conventions are shown in the demo outputs; the benchmark key grades against the course convention, Re = ρvh/μ. Ca, the capillary number, compares viscous to surface-tension forces — at normal writing Ca ≈ 0.002, so surface tension dominates, as expected.

Why flex gives 4×, not 8×

Doubling h multiplies the slit's conductance by 2³ = 8. But in a real pen the driving pressure is capillary, and ΔPcap is proportional to 1/h — so doubling the gap halves the push at the same moment it opens the channel. Net effect: 8 × ½ = 4× the flow (3.17 → 12.7 µL/s). Any model — human or AI — that holds ΔP fixed while flexing the nib overstates the thick stroke by a factor of two. The fixed-pressure mode in the demo exists precisely to expose that difference; it is only physical for a pressurised cartridge.

Why pens stop: vacuum lock

Capillary suction can only deliver ink if air can get back into the reservoir to replace it. If the feed seals completely, a partial vacuum builds until it cancels ΔPcap — and a pen full of ink writes nothing. Same equation, supply side.

Assumptions — stated, not hidden

  • Steady, incompressible, Newtonian flow
  • Fully developed laminar flow between parallel plates
  • All inputs in SI units inside the calculation (microns never appear in the maths — the µm→m conversion happens once, at the slider)
  • Contact angle enters only through cos θ
  • Gravity head uses a fixed 1 cm ink column
  • Ink treated as near-Newtonian, which holds for the dye-based inks this project considers; pigmented non-Newtonian inks are out of scope

Units trap

The single most damaging implementation error is writing 30 µm as 30×10⁻³ m instead of 30×10⁻⁶ m. That makes h itself 1,000× too large — and because Q grows with h³, the flow rate comes out 1,000³ = a billion times too big. The benchmark suite below includes this trap deliberately.

What we learned — and what's next

From the fluid mechanics

  • Surface tension is not a footnote at small scales — shrink a channel to 30 µm and it outweighs gravity 29 to 1. Scale decides which forces matter.
  • An equation and its validity range are a package deal. The Re check is part of the answer, and “the model refuses to answer” is sometimes the correct output.
  • Coupled effects beat single-factor intuition: the h³ law alone predicts 8× for the flex pen; coupling it to ΔPcap ∝ 1/h gives the true 4×.
  • Unit discipline is a physical skill, not bookkeeping — a µm/m slip is cubed by the h³ term.

From working with AI models

  • A precise spec (units, formulas to use and to avoid, edge cases named in advance) is most of the work; the code is the easy part.
  • Models are most dangerous when they are fluent and wrong — extrapolating a laminar formula into turbulent flow with full confidence. Benchmarks with known answers are the antidote.
  • A second model is a genuinely useful reviewer for scope and missing pieces, but its physics claims need the same verification as the first model's code.

Worth learning next

  • Ink wicking into paper (Washburn equation) — the second half of line formation, after the nib delivers the ink.
  • Non-Newtonian and pigmented inks, and how shear-thinning changes the slit-flow result.
  • Feed design: how real fountain-pen feeds use fin geometry to buffer flow and prevent vacuum lock.
